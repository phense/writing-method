# Story quality and prose quality

Use when evaluating a premise, pilot or revision. These are provisional editorial questions from a four-work comparison, not a universal scoring model. Distinguish the intended reader experience from the author's taste and from defects such as broken causality. A pleasant domestic scene and a thriller sequence need different rhythms.

Provenance: own comparative analysis from private studies of existing works; the private study notes are not distributed. See the [source index](../../../shared/story/sources.md#study-derived-references).

## Story: desire, choice, consequence

Start with [storytelling rule #1](../../../shared/story/rules.md#1-storytelling-rule-therefore--but): can the major beats be linked through "therefore" consequences or "but" complications and responses? Locate any transition that is merely "and then" and identify the missing motivation or consequence before sentence-level revision. This is a check on the events, not a connective-word quota.

Ask what someone wants here, what prevents an easy outcome, what they choose and what remains changed. Powerful characters can lack information, trust, access, judgment or belonging without secretly losing their established abilities. Supporting characters need consequential choices when the story presents them as agents; admiration alone is not development.

A scene may do external and relational work together: solving a practical problem can change trust or obligation. This is an opportunity, not a quota. Rest, atmosphere and shared pleasure can themselves fulfill the chosen promise. Judge quiet passages by their purpose rather than a mandatory conflict count.

Trace a distinctive world feature through lived consequence. A new power, institution or species should change possibilities, expectations or perception somewhere meaningful. Avoid demanding that every decorative detail justify a plot turn. Keep the intended ending visible through the choices that matter along the way.

## Prose: attention, meaning, duration

Before polishing, name viewpoint, immediate intention and intended effect. Select details through this observer's knowledge and values. Different voices notice and misjudge different things; a vocabulary swap alone does not individualize them.

Check whether the reader can locate action, identify speakers and understand references without resolving a deliberate mystery. Use precise nouns and verbs when they clarify; retain abstraction or indirectness when the narrator deliberately uses it. Do not enforce short sentences, sensory quotas or “show, never tell.”

Use sentence and paragraph length to allocate experienced time. Compression can carry routine; a decisive small reaction may deserve space. A short response after a longer explanation can change interpretation, but copying the rhythm everywhere makes it predictable.

Distinguish a revealing narrator comment from a redundant explanation. Temporarily remove the comment and inspect what becomes unclear or newly ambiguous; restore it when it contributes voice, orientation or a necessary reading. Do not automatically delete all explicit emotion.

For dialogue, identify what each participant seeks, assumes and withholds. Affection, reassurance and shared play count as purposes. Preserve subtext only when the reader has enough evidence to perceive it; unexplained vagueness is not depth.

## Revision and feedback

First repair missing motivation, causal jumps and mismatched promises. Then adjust scene order, emphasis and information. Polish diction and rhythm after those changes, and verify canon consistency before accepting new facts.

Select one or two concrete reader questions for the current pass. Compare an original and revision against those questions without calling a deliberately weak contrast an unbiased improvement test. Record author feedback separately from model judgments. A clean rubric, a preferred sample and a successful pilot establish different things; none alone proves novel-length quality.
